## Capabilities it proposed

### "“Start meeting mode.”"
- **useful because:** With one pendant press or spoken command, the hive should recognize the current/next meeting, quietly assemble the agenda and relevant mail/calendar context, keep a live 24 kHz audio sidecar while the owner talks, and after the meeting leave a cited recap with decisions, open questions, and draft follow-ups—never sending anything automatically. The pendant supplies an immediate, hands-free trigger and audio; the Mac knows which meeting app/window is active and can read Calendar/Mail; the browser bridge can inspect the authenticated agenda or project tab; the relay keeps the session alive and delivers the recap when the Mac or pendant link briefly drops.
- **path:** pendant → relay-realtime → mac-planner → browser-extension → dashboard
- **model tier:** Realtime model only for the live turn-taking/audio control; use a cheaper background model for agenda extraction, transcript cleanup, decision/action-item synthesis, and draft generation.
- **latency:** Acknowledge the pendant trigger in under 300 ms, select the meeting context in under 2 s, and keep audio playout below 250 ms end-to-end. Post-meeting recap can take 1–3 minutes and should be resumable.
- **cost:** One realtime session plus roughly 2–5 minutes of background transcription/synthesis; dominant cost is live audio tokens, so only the meeting interval should use realtime and the recap should use the slower tier.
- **security:** Calendar/Mail and authenticated browser content leave the Mac only as redacted, cited excerpts; raw audio/transcript needs explicit retention (default delete after recap). Never submit/send a draft. Starting capture must be an explicit button or spoken command with a clear LED/audio acknowledgment; meeting mode must show an obvious active indicator.
- **missing:** A meeting-context correlator joining the next Calendar event to the foreground Mac app and optionally an authenticated browser tab; A session-scoped audio/transcript capsule that survives a dropped Mac link and supports resumable post-meeting processing; A citation-preserving recap/draft artifact written to the owner's AI-Pendant-Workspace with a dashboard review state; A true 24 kHz capture/uplink path: current pendant capture is 15,625 Hz and 16 kHz Opus uplink even though playback decodes 24 kHz

### "“Move this conversation to my Mac.”"
- **useful because:** The owner should be able to begin a thought hands-free on the pendant and continue it at the desk without repeating the request or losing the assistant's working context. The pendant marks the exact turn and provides a short spoken acknowledgment; the relay seals a resumable conversation capsule; the Mac opens the relevant workspace, browser tab, or draft and presents the same pending decision; the owner can then type, inspect sources, or continue by voice. When they leave the desk, a single pendant press should hand the unfinished state back, including what was already done and what remains.
- **path:** pendant → relay-realtime → mac-planner → browser-extension → dashboard
- **model tier:** Realtime only for the handoff acknowledgment and any continuing live speech. Use a cheaper background model to compress the conversation capsule, select relevant files/tabs, and generate the Mac-side continuation view.
- **latency:** Acknowledge in under 300 ms, persist the capsule before replying, and have the Mac workspace ready within 3 seconds. Returning to the pendant should require only a short state summary, not a full replay.
- **cost:** One small realtime turn per handoff; background compression and workspace selection should be a low-cost model. Storage is a small encrypted capsule plus references, so the dominant cost is not storage but repeated context if the capsule is not compact.
- **security:** The capsule may contain private speech, authenticated URLs, and draft text. Encrypt it, retain it only until the task is completed or for a user-configurable short TTL, and redact secrets from dashboard previews. Opening a file or tab is reversible; sending, deleting, purchasing, or submitting must remain an explicit separate action. The owner must see which Mac surfaces will be opened before the handoff executes.
- **missing:** A versioned, encrypted handoff-capsule format with turn boundary, task state, completed actions, pending decisions, source references, and expiry; A relay-to-Mac handoff route with idempotency and acknowledgement, plus a Mac-to-pendant resume route; A workspace resolver that maps capsule references to an existing Mac file/tab without leaking authenticated page contents into the relay log; A dashboard/menubar continuation card showing capsule provenance and allowing discard or resume


## Changes it proposed to its own stack

### `firmware` — Make the audio contract genuinely symmetric at 24 kHz: capture 24 kHz from the microphone (or oversample at 48 kHz and decimate), encode 24 kHz Opus uplink, and advertise the negotiated sample rate in the pipeline handshake. Keep the current 24 kHz decoder for downlink, but add bounded jitter buffering and a relay pass-through mode so the relay does not silently transcode 16 kHz speech back into 24 kHz. Measure encode+decode CPU, heap high-water, packet loss, and glass-to-glass latency in a hardware-in-the-loop fixture; refuse the mode and fall back to 16 kHz before audio underruns if the budget is exceeded.
- **owner gets:** The owner's voice and the assistant's reply would stop being upsampled on one direction: meeting mode, voice notes, and ordinary conversation would sound noticeably more natural and intelligible, especially for names and consonants. A negotiated fallback means a weak link becomes lower fidelity rather than a broken conversation.
- effort: Medium-to-high: firmware microphone clock/driver and Opus application mode, relay negotiation/transcode changes, bridge clock validation, and a repeatable HIL latency/packet-loss test. The current nRF9160 prototype has only 211,608 B app RAM and both encode/decode already consume about 87% of one core, so profiling must precede enabling it by default; the eventual product board may need a codec/DSP or faster MCU.  ·  risk: 24 kHz capture can exhaust CPU, increase radio bitrate and battery drain, or expose clock drift between the nRF9160 and ESP32. Recover by capability negotiation, a watchdog on underrun, and automatic 16 kHz fallback; retain diagnostic counters rather than dropping the session.
- cost: No incremental API cost for codec changes; 24 kHz Opus likely increases radio airtime and energy roughly in proportion to bitrate. Hardware cost is TBD, but a production audio codec/DSP or faster MCU may add roughly $3–$10 BOM and tens of mW compared with this development kit.  ·  latency: Potentially +10–30 ms from larger capture/codec work unless frames stay bounded; target under 250 ms glass-to-glass and measure p50/p95. Fallback must happen within one failed negotiation/underrun window.
- security: Audio remains encrypted in transit; higher-fidelity raw audio increases sensitivity and should use the existing short-retention/deletion policy. Do not persist PCM on the pendant or relay except bounded diagnostic samples with explicit opt-in.
- depends on: A pipeline handshake carrying sample rate/codec/bitrate and a relay pass-through path; The pending audio-path diagnostic/HIL fixture and offline delivery retry behavior; A production hardware decision if nRF9160 CPU headroom cannot meet simultaneous encode/decode; An end-to-end 24 kHz capture test using the actual microphone clock rather than resampling a 16 kHz source

### `hardware` — Replace the prototype nRF9160-plus-ESP32 audio split with a production pendant audio front end: a low-power 24/48 kHz codec or DSP with hardware sample-rate conversion, a small secure ring-buffer SRAM, and a shared clock exposed to the modem MCU. Keep the nRF9160 for LTE-M/NB-IoT control and negotiation, but move Opus pre/post-processing and jitter-buffer servicing off the 64 MHz application core. Add a physical recording-status indicator driven independently of firmware task load.
- **owner gets:** They would get natural, stable 24 kHz speech without the pendant becoming hot, stuttering, or dying quickly, and the recording indicator would remain trustworthy even if the network or application task is wedged. It makes long conversations and meeting handoffs dependable rather than a lab-only mode.
- effort: High: select codec/DSP and PMIC, redesign the board and clock tree, update Zephyr drivers and the ESP32 bridge contract, validate RF coexistence and acoustic performance, and manufacture a small pilot. This is intentionally a product redesign rather than a software workaround for the development kit.  ·  risk: New clock domains can introduce drift, RF noise, boot failures, or a more difficult firmware update path. Recover with a bypass path to the existing I2S wiring, a hardware watchdog, signed dual-image firmware, and a 16 kHz compatibility mode. Validate on a pilot batch before replacing the prototype fleet.
- cost: Roughly $5–$15 additional BOM depending on codec/DSP, SRAM, PMIC, and indicator choices, plus one-time board/NRE cost; active audio power should fall versus spending most of the nRF9160 core on codec work, though idle BOM power rises slightly.  ·  latency: Hardware buffering should reduce codec CPU contention and make p95 latency more predictable; target under 50 ms local audio buffering and under 250 ms glass-to-glass. Clock conversion must be bounded and measurable.
- security: A hardware ring buffer must be volatile by default and zeroized on stop; do not add persistent raw-audio storage. The independent indicator must be fail-safe—if capture power is enabled, it should indicate capture even when the main MCU is unresponsive.
- depends on: A measured 24 kHz end-to-end audio contract and HIL test vector; A product-board decision replacing the explicitly provisional Nordic development kit; Relay negotiation for codec/sample-rate capability and a bridge firmware protocol version; A hardware privacy-indicator review with the owner
