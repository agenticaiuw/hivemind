## Capabilities it proposed

### "“Keep track of the things I promise people—including things I say casually—and, at the right time, remind me with the context I need; let me mark it done or prepare the follow-up without sending anything.”"
- **useful because:** People lose commitments between a spoken aside, a calendar event, an email thread, and a private web task. The pendant can catch the promise at the moment it is made; the relay can preserve it when the Mac is asleep; the Mac and authenticated browser can later find supporting context and verify completion. This is a closed loop rather than another reminder: it distinguishes a promise from a thought, asks only when ambiguity matters, and gives a sourced next action without silently contacting anyone.
- **path:** pendant → relay-realtime → mac-planner → browser-extension → unified → faculty-perception → faculty-judgement → faculty-action
- **model tier:** Use the realtime tier only to detect/clarify a commitment during conversation. Use a cheaper background model for nightly reconciliation, evidence extraction, deadline prediction, and drafting. Keep pendant-side capture deterministic and offline-capable.
- **latency:** Capture acknowledgement under 500 ms locally/relay-side; no blocking lookup during conversation. Reconciliation can run in 1–5 minutes after connectivity and a quiet-hours-aware check-in can arrive at the next useful moment.
- **cost:** About $0.01–$0.05 per commitment lifecycle, dominated by background synthesis and authenticated-page extraction; most spoken acknowledgements should avoid a model call. Storage is small text plus provenance; browser screenshots should be opt-in and short-lived.
- **security:** Commitments may contain sensitive names, health, or work data. Store a redacted commitment and encrypted provenance, with per-source sensitivity and TTL. Reading logged-in mail/browser pages is allowed by the owner's policy, but sending mail, submitting forms, deleting data, or changing a calendar item requires explicit approval. Every reminder should show why it believes the commitment is open and which evidence it used.
- **missing:** A durable commitment/obligation ledger with tentative versus confirmed state, owner-visible provenance, due-window and recurrence fields, deduplication, and completion/snooze semantics.; A cross-surface reconciliation worker that can join pendant utterances to Calendar/Mail/Notes/browser evidence without copying whole pages into prompts.; A local interruption gate and pendant delivery acknowledgement so a reminder is deferred safely when the owner is driving, speaking, or offline.; An approval-aware action compiler that turns a confirmed follow-up into a previewable draft and receipt.

### "“When I leave home, work, or another place I choose, quietly check where I’m going next and tell me what I need to bring, finish, or remember before I get there.”"
- **useful because:** A calendar reminder says where an appointment is; it does not notice that the owner is actually leaving, reconcile the destination with open work and private web tasks, or catch the one item left behind. The pendant can provide the moment-of-departure signal, the relay can remain available when the Mac sleeps, and the Mac/browser can assemble a short, evidence-backed departure checklist from calendar, mail, notes, and logged-in reservation or work pages. This is proactive physical-world context, not another scheduled briefing.
- **path:** pendant → relay-realtime → mac-planner → browser-extension → unified → faculty-perception → faculty-judgement → faculty-action
- **model tier:** Use deterministic geofence and calendar matching first. Use a cheaper background model to compose and rank the checklist. Reserve realtime only for ambiguous spoken corrections such as “I’m not going to the office after all.”
- **latency:** A departure event should produce a useful checklist within 5–15 seconds, with a local fallback such as “You have an appointment nearby.” Background page reconciliation may take up to two minutes and must never delay leaving.
- **cost:** Usually below $0.01 per departure when calendar and cached context suffice; $0.02–$0.08 when authenticated browser extraction or synthesis is needed. GNSS checks and LTE-M bursts add battery cost, so use motion-triggered sampling and coarse geofences rather than continuous tracking.
- **security:** Location history is highly sensitive. Keep exact coordinates on-device, send only a named place and coarse destination when possible, expire departure events quickly, and provide a hardware-button or voice disable mode. Browser/mail content must remain source-scoped and cited. Never send a message, alter a reservation, or buy anything from the checklist without explicit confirmation.
- **missing:** Pendant-side GNSS/geofence and motion-trigger support, with coarse-place derivation that works during a dropped LTE link.; A privacy-preserving place profile store: named places, entry/exit rules, retention limits, and an owner-visible audit trail.; A destination resolver that joins place events to calendar and relevant open threads, then ranks only time-critical checklist items.; A local checklist cache and delivery acknowledgement so the pendant can speak a short fallback even when the Mac, browser bridge, or relay is unavailable.


## Changes it proposed to its own stack

### `hardware` — Replace the prototype’s 15,625 Hz I2S microphone/clock path with a production audio front end that captures 24 kHz (preferably a 48 kHz digital MEMS mic plus deterministic decimation, or a native 24 kHz I2S clock), and carry an explicit audio-format contract through firmware, relay transcoding, and Mac playback. Keep 24 kHz Opus decode on playback, but stop advertising the path as superwideband when uplink capture is physically limited to 15,625 Hz. Add an end-to-end test fixture that records a swept 8–12 kHz signal and verifies sample rate, timestamps, packet loss concealment, and intelligibility at the pendant.
- **owner gets:** The owner gets genuinely clearer speech and more reliable recognition of names and consonants in noisy everyday use, rather than a 24 kHz label applied to narrowband input. It also makes the wearable’s answer sound consistent in both directions and exposes failures before they become confusing conversations.
- effort: High: select and layout a mic/clock, update nRF9160 I2S/DMA and Opus framing, update relay resampling/metadata, and run acoustic plus LTE-M power validation. Treat the current Nordic DK as a bring-up fixture, not the final enclosure.  ·  risk: A clock/DMA mismatch can produce drift, buffer overruns, or dropped packets; higher-rate capture may expose the already-heavy CPU budget (encode ~15 ms per call and encode+decode about 87% of one core). Roll out behind a negotiated format bit and retain the current 16 kHz uplink fallback. If LTE-M power or CPU is unacceptable, capture at 48 kHz locally but downsample to a verified 24 kHz frame before encoding.
- cost: Roughly $3–$15 incremental BOM for a better digital mic/clock and power regulation in volume (more for a redesigned board); modest additional LTE-M airtime and RAM/DMA buffers, plus engineering/test cost. API cost is unchanged if transcoding stays deterministic.  ·  latency: Potentially +5–15 ms for resampling and larger buffers; preserve 60 ms Opus framing and negotiate down to 16 kHz on weak links.
- security: No new data class, but higher-fidelity audio can reveal more ambient detail. Keep existing transport encryption, avoid retaining raw PCM, and make format negotiation and recording retention observable in receipts.
- depends on: A verified 24 kHz end-to-end acceptance test and explicit format metadata in POST /pipeline/audio and GET /pipeline/:pipelineId/audio/:direction; Firmware CPU/RAM and LTE-M power measurements on the target wearable, not only the development kit; Relay and Mac playback updates that reject mislabeled sample rates instead of silently resampling twice

### `hardware` — Add a local departure-signal subsystem to the wearable: GNSS-capable location sampling with geofence evaluation and a low-power motion trigger, keeping exact coordinates in volatile/on-device storage and emitting only a named/coarse place-transition event upstream. Include a physical privacy-disable gesture and a bounded local checklist cache for disconnected operation.
- **owner gets:** The owner can receive a timely, private “before you leave” checklist without carrying the phone or remembering to ask. It catches forgotten items and preparation steps at the only moment they are still fixable, while avoiding a permanent location trail in the cloud.
- effort: High: select or enable GNSS on the nRF9160 design, characterize acquisition and LTE-M coexistence, implement geofence/motion firmware, privacy controls, encrypted local state, and relay event semantics. Product enclosure and antenna redesign may be required.  ·  risk: GNSS can be slow indoors, inaccurate in cities, and power-hungry; false departures could annoy the owner. Use hysteresis, motion confirmation, named-place confidence, quiet hours, and a local disable latch. If GNSS is unavailable, fall back to owner-triggered place changes and never claim certainty.
- cost: Approximately $2–$10 incremental BOM if the modem/antenna can be reused, potentially more for RF and enclosure redesign. GNSS acquisition adds intermittent power draw and may increase LTE-M airtime only when a place transition is confirmed.  ·  latency: Cold fixes may take tens of seconds, so motion-triggered prediction and cached geofences should provide an immediate provisional event; final confirmation can arrive later. No impact on ordinary voice latency.
- security: Materially increases the sensitivity of the device. Exact coordinates must not leave the pendant by default; retain only coarse place IDs and short-lived transition records upstream. Privacy disable must be locally enforceable even with a compromised relay.
- depends on: A typed place-transition event contract shared by pendant, relay, Mac, and dashboard; A durable privacy policy for location retention and source disclosure; A local checklist cache and delivery acknowledgement protocol; A destination/context joiner that consumes the coarse event without requiring raw coordinates
